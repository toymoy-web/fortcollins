Sump Pumps and Battery Backup: What Fort Collins Basements Actually Need.

By the Fort Collins 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated September 2026.

A sump pump is the difference between a wet basement and a flooded one during a heavy Fort Collins storm, but not every home needs the same setup. The right sump pump investment depends heavily on where your specific property sits relative to the city's documented flood risk, and treating every basement identically means either underinvesting somewhere genuinely exposed or overspending somewhere that doesn't need it.

For homes in Alta Vista, Buckingham, or other documented lower-lying neighborhoods, a working sump pump with reliable battery backup isn't an optional upgrade, it's a genuinely higher-stakes investment given the real, official floodplain status these areas carry. The reasoning is straightforward: a power outage during a major storm is exactly the scenario when a standard pump fails you, and that's precisely the situation a flood-exposed home is most likely to face during Fort Collins' peak June-through-August storm season.

Pump capacity matters as much as backup power. A pump sized for an average property isn't necessarily adequate for a home with a genuinely higher water table or more direct river-corridor exposure. Signs your current pump might be undersized include it running constantly rather than cycling normally, which can point to either a failing check valve or a pump simply overwhelmed by more water inflow than it was built to handle.

A pump that isn't turning on during an actual storm is a different, more urgent problem, usually a float switch failure, which is often a straightforward and inexpensive fix once diagnosed. Testing this before you need it matters more than most homeowners realize: pouring a bucket of water into the basin every few months to confirm the float switch triggers the pump properly catches most failures well before an actual storm puts it to the test.

Battery backup specifically deserves its own check, separate from the pump itself. A backup battery that's quietly died sitting unused defeats the entire purpose of having one, and this is easy to overlook since the battery only matters during the exact power-outage scenario it's meant to protect against. Our sump pump installation and repair page covers both new installation sizing and how we evaluate an existing system's actual capacity.

If a storm does get past your sump pump, whether from an undersized system, a failed battery, or simply more water than any residential pump could handle, the next step is fast, moisture-verified extraction and drying, not a wait-and-see approach. Our water damage restoration team responds 24/7 specifically because speed matters enormously once water is already inside.

This isn't purely a Fort Collins city-limits issue either. LaPorte's proximity to the Cache la Poudre River means some properties there carry real flood exposure too, even alongside the well-and-septic considerations that define so much of LaPorte's plumbing picture.

If you're installing a sump pump for the first time rather than replacing or upgrading an existing one, it's worth having a conversation about basin sizing specifically, not just pump horsepower. A pump that's more than adequate on paper still underperforms if the basin itself is too small to allow the float switch room to cycle properly, which is a common installation mistake that shows up as a pump that seems to run constantly even though its rated capacity should be more than sufficient.

For homeowners who've never had a water event and are wondering whether this is worth addressing proactively, the honest answer depends entirely on your specific property's documented flood-zone status. A home well outside any floodplain designation, on higher ground, genuinely faces less urgency here than one in a documented risk zone, and it's worth checking your specific status before deciding how much to invest.

Whatever your current setup, an annual test before the June-through-August storm season begins is a reasonable habit regardless of your property's specific risk level. It takes a few minutes and catches the majority of failures before they matter, which is a much better time to discover a problem than during an actual storm.

It's also worth having a backup plan beyond the pump itself for a genuinely high-risk property. A water alarm sensor placed near the basin, separate from the pump system entirely, gives you an early warning even if the pump itself fails for a reason the float-switch test wouldn't catch, giving you a chance to respond before water spreads significantly.

For homeowners who've inherited a sump pump system from a previous owner with no documentation of its age or service history, treating it as an unknown quantity rather than assuming it's adequate is the more cautious and honest approach. A quick evaluation early in your ownership, before you're relying on it during an actual storm, tells you exactly what you're working with.

There's also a maintenance rhythm worth establishing beyond the occasional bucket test. Cleaning debris out of the basin itself once or twice a year keeps the pump's intake clear, since accumulated silt and small debris can restrict flow even when the pump's motor and float switch are both functioning correctly. This is a five-minute task that's easy to forget precisely because the system usually works fine without it, right up until a heavy storm demands full capacity and the restricted intake becomes the limiting factor.

Ultimately, the right sump pump investment for your home is the one matched honestly to your property's actual documented risk, not the cheapest option available or the most expensive one a salesperson recommends. A direct evaluation of your basin, your neighborhood's flood history, and your current pump's real condition is the only way to get that match right.

It's also worth checking whether your homeowner's insurance policy offers any discount or requirement tied to sump pump backup systems, since some carriers do factor this into flood-adjacent coverage terms. A quick call to your agent alongside your plumbing evaluation can sometimes reveal a cost offset you weren't otherwise aware of, making the investment a bit more straightforward financially. Some policies also specify minimum backup requirements for full flood-damage coverage, which is worth confirming before you assume your current setup automatically qualifies. Finding this out before a claim, rather than during one, is the entire point of asking now, since a denied claim over a technicality is far more frustrating than a five-minute phone call would have been beforehand. That single call is a small amount of effort for the certainty of knowing your coverage actually matches your real setup.

Common Questions.

How do I know if my sump pump is actually undersized?

A technician can evaluate your basin, drainage inflow, and current pump capacity against your home's actual risk level to confirm whether it's adequate.

Can a sump pump be installed in a basement that doesn't currently have one?

Yes, new installations, including cutting a basin if needed, are a routine part of our work in the older, lower-lying neighborhoods.
