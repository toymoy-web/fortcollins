Galvanized Pipe in Older Fort Collins Homes: Signs It's Time to Repipe.

By the Fort Collins 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated September 2026.

A lot of homes in Fort Collins' older neighborhoods, particularly those built before the 1970s, still run original galvanized steel supply lines. Galvanized pipe was standard practice for decades, and it isn't automatically a problem just because it's old. The issue is what happens as it ages: galvanized steel corrodes from the inside, gradually restricting flow and eventually developing pinhole leaks, often in more than one spot as the pipe reaches the end of its practical service life.

The tricky part is that this corrosion happens invisibly, inside the pipe, long before it shows up as an obvious symptom. By the time a homeowner notices reduced water pressure or a visible leak, the underlying corrosion has usually been progressing for years..

Recurring pinhole leaks in different, unrelated spots are the clearest sign the pipe material itself, not just one bad section, is the actual problem. If you've had two separate leaks in different parts of your home within a couple of years, that pattern is worth paying attention to rather than treating each leak as an isolated incident.

Rusty or discolored water from multiple fixtures is another reliable indicator, since it points to corrosion happening broadly throughout the system rather than a single failed part. A gradual, noticeable drop in water pressure over months, easy to write off as normal aging, is also a real symptom of galvanized pipe's interior diameter narrowing as corrosion builds up.

If you can see exposed pipe in your basement or crawlspace, galvanized pipe typically has a dull gray appearance and may show visible rust streaking, worth checking even before any symptoms appear. Our repiping and whole-home pipe replacement page covers what a full or phased repipe project actually involves once you've confirmed this is the situation.

It's worth being honest about timing here. Repiping is a genuinely bigger investment than most plumbing repairs, and we won't recommend it the moment we see any galvanized pipe. The honest threshold is usually once a home has had more than one or two unrelated leaks, since at that point continuing to chase individual repairs typically costs more over time than addressing the underlying pipe material directly. If a specific section is actively failing before that broader pattern emerges, our water line repair and replacement service handles that targeted fix.

This is much more of an issue in Fort Collins' and Loveland's older housing stock than in a newer-construction market. Homes built from the 1970s onward, and certainly anything built in the last two decades, almost universally use copper or PEX rather than galvanized steel, so this specific concern doesn't apply the same way to more recently built properties.

Cost is naturally the first question most homeowners have once repiping comes up as a real possibility rather than a distant hypothetical. A full whole-home repipe is a meaningfully larger investment than a single leak repair, which is exactly why phased approaches exist, tackling the worst-affected sections of the home first and spreading the remaining work out as budget allows, rather than requiring the entire cost upfront.

If you're on the fence about whether your specific situation has crossed the threshold from repairable to genuinely repipe-worthy, a straightforward inspection is the honest way to find out rather than guessing. A technician can check accessible sections, review your home's known leak history, and test pressure and flow at multiple fixtures to give you a real answer rather than a generic age-based estimate.

It's also worth knowing that not every older home with some galvanized pipe needs a full repipe immediately. If your home has original galvanized lines but no history of leaks and normal water pressure throughout, that's a reasonable situation to monitor rather than act on right away. The recurring-leak pattern, not the pipe's age alone, is what actually signals it's time.

One more consideration worth raising during any inspection: galvanized pipe interacts differently with copper than modern materials do, and a home with a mix of original galvanized and a partial past repair using copper can develop galvanic corrosion at the connection point between the two metals. This is a specific, identifiable failure pattern a technician will check for directly if your home has had any piecemeal repairs over the years rather than a single consistent pipe material throughout.

Financing is worth asking about directly if cost is the main barrier to addressing a confirmed repipe need. A full whole-home repipe is a genuinely significant expense, and understanding your realistic payment options upfront, whether that's a phased approach or another arrangement, helps you make a decision based on the actual plumbing need rather than delaying purely because the full cost feels overwhelming all at once.

It's worth remembering, too, that a repipe is one of the few plumbing investments that genuinely improves a home's resale prospects, not just its day-to-day reliability. A documented, recent repipe is a selling point buyers specifically ask about in older neighborhoods, since it removes exactly the uncertainty a buyer would otherwise be inheriting along with the rest of the home's history.

For a homeowner still deciding whether it's the right time, the honest test remains the same one worth applying throughout: has your home had more than one unrelated leak recently, and are you tired of wondering when the next one will show up. If the answer to either is yes, it's a reasonable time to have the conversation.

It's also worth thinking about timing beyond just the plumbing itself. If you're already planning a kitchen or bathroom remodel that involves opening walls in areas with known galvanized runs, that's a natural, cost-effective window to address the pipe material at the same time, rather than closing the wall back up and facing the same decision again later once the finishes are already in place. Coordinating the two projects also means you only disrupt the affected rooms once, rather than twice on two separate occasions years apart. Mentioning the pipe material to your remodeling contractor early in planning gives everyone enough lead time to scope it properly rather than treating it as a last-minute addition that throws off the project's budget and schedule unexpectedly and creates friction between trades on site, particularly if walls already need to be reopened after being closed the first time around.

That small bit of upfront coordination is usually the difference between a smooth remodel and an unplanned second visit to reopen the same wall.

FAQs.

Common Questions.

How do I know for sure if I have galvanized pipe?

A technician can identify it during an inspection, or you can check an exposed section yourself, typically in a basement or crawlspace, since it has a distinctive dull gray look.

Is a phased repipe a real option, or does it have to be all at once?

Phased repiping, tackling the worst sections first, is a reasonable option when a full repipe isn't in the budget right now.
