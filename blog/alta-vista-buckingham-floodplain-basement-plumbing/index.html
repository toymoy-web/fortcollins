Why Alta Vista and Buckingham Homes Still Sit in the Floodplain

Alta Vista and Buckingham are two of Fort Collins' oldest neighborhoods, and they share something that isn't just historical color: both remain inside the city's official 100-year floodplain today, according to Fort Collins' own 2005 Northside Neighborhoods Plan. That's a current planning designation, not a reference to a past event.

The reason traces back to how these neighborhoods were originally built. Working-class immigrant communities, including the historically documented Andersonville and Tres Colonias areas, were built low near the Cache la Poudre River and its tributaries because that was the available, affordable land at the time. Wealthier postwar development elsewhere in the city, like Sheely, was deliberately built up on higher ground specifically to avoid the flood exposure these older neighborhoods still carry.

For a homeowner in Alta Vista or Buckingham today, this history has a genuinely practical implication for basement plumbing decisions. A basement plumbing project in either neighborhood, whether that's a new bathroom rough-in or fixing a floor drain that backs up during storms, needs to account for real flood exposure in a way that a similar project in a higher-ground neighborhood wouldn't.

Ejector pump systems become more than a code requirement in this context. If you're finishing a basement bathroom in Alta Vista or Buckingham, the system needs to handle not just routine wastewater but the increased water table pressure that comes with genuine floodplain proximity. Our basement plumbing page covers what that rough-in actually involves.

Sump pump capacity matters even more here than in a typical Fort Collins home. A pump sized for an average property isn't necessarily sized for one in a documented floodplain, and the battery backup question isn't optional the way it might be elsewhere in the city. Our sump pump installation and repair page walks through what proper sizing looks like for this specific risk profile.

It's worth comparing this to a town like Windsor, which has grown almost entirely through newer construction built to current flood-aware standards. Windsor homeowners generally don't face this same historical floodplain exposure, simply because most of the town's housing postdates the planning standards that would have prevented building there in the first place.

None of this is meant to alarm Alta Vista or Buckingham homeowners unnecessarily. Plenty of homes in both neighborhoods have never taken on water. The point is that if you have noticed water intrusion, or you're planning basement work, the floodplain designation is a real, documented fact worth factoring into the plumbing decisions you make, not background noise to ignore.

If you're buying a home in either neighborhood, this is worth raising directly during inspection rather than discovering it after closing. Ask specifically whether the property has a documented flood history, what the existing sump pump setup actually consists of, and whether the basement has ever been finished with an ejector pump system rather than gravity drainage. These are answerable questions, and getting honest answers upfront is far cheaper than finding out the hard way during the first heavy summer storm.

For homeowners who've owned in Alta Vista or Buckingham for years without incident, it's worth resisting the temptation to assume that track record means the risk has passed. Flood exposure tied to a documented floodplain designation doesn't diminish with time the way some risks do. A dry decade doesn't change the underlying elevation and drainage realities that put these neighborhoods in the floodplain in the first place.

The good news is that addressing this risk doesn't require dramatic or expensive intervention in most cases. A properly sized sump pump with battery backup, combined with a basement plumbing setup that accounts for the area's water table, covers the overwhelming majority of what a homeowner in either neighborhood actually needs to be protected.

It's also worth having a conversation with neighbors who've lived on the same block for a longer stretch of time, if you're new to either area. Longtime residents often have direct, practical knowledge about which specific corners of a block have historically taken on water and which haven't, information that's more granular than a citywide floodplain map can offer and genuinely useful when deciding how much to invest in protection.

If you're planning a larger renovation in either neighborhood beyond just basement work, it's worth having the home's overall drainage and grading evaluated as part of the same project rather than treating plumbing and exterior drainage as unrelated concerns. In floodplain-adjacent areas specifically, the two are more connected than they might initially seem, since exterior water management directly affects how much stress your interior systems face during a storm.

The overarching takeaway for both neighborhoods is straightforward even if the underlying history is complex: a documented floodplain designation is a fact worth building your plumbing decisions around, from sump pump sizing to basement rough-in planning, rather than a piece of trivia to set aside once you've read about it once.

For homeowners in either neighborhood who've never had a professional evaluate their current flood readiness, that's a reasonable next step regardless of whether you've had a past issue. A direct assessment gives you a clear, honest picture of exactly where you actually stand, rather than relying on assumptions about a home you may not have fully evaluated since moving in.

In many cases yes, particularly if the existing rough-in plumbing is sound and only the pump itself needs upgrading.
